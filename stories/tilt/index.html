You've lost three in a row.
Should you stop?

Every chess player knows the feeling: a couple of losses, and the next game already feels lost before it starts. Tilt has a name, endless forum threads devoted to it, and a standard piece of advice attached to it, usually some version of "stop after three losses". Many players have strong opinions about tilt, but what does the data actually say?

To find out, I took the FlawChess benchmark database, 2.67 million rated Lichess games from 4,487 players across bullet, blitz, rapid and classical, lined up each player's games in order, and looked at what happens in the game after a losing streak. The short version: your own stats overstate tilt badly and what is left once you compare like with like is small.

1

Your stats say tilt is huge. They are wrong.

#

Let's start with the naive version of the question: line up each player's games and look at the one right after a streak. In the 2.67 million games here, players average a 43% score in the game after six or more straight losses, and 65% after six or more straight wins. That is a 22-point swing, and it is the kind of number articles about tilt usually report: one analysis of 840,000 Lichess games puts the next game at 39% after five straight losses and 58% after five straight wins, and a regression on a million games finds that even the result from seven games back still predicts the current one. Almost none of it survives four checks:

  • Opponents. A long streak is mostly a story about who you were playing. The pairing system barely moves (six straight wins lift your rating by about 30 points), but half of all streaks of six or more were played against opponents averaging more than 100 points weaker: arenas, fresh accounts, the thin top of the pool, a friend who keeps accepting. The next opponent comes from the same place, on average 175 rating points weaker after six or more wins and 61 points stronger after six or more losses. Keep only next games against an opponent within 100 points and the swing shrinks to 48–54%.
  • The calendar. Seven in ten streaks of six or more span more than one sitting (a break of an hour or more starts a new sitting), and four in ten more than a day. A bad mood can certainly last that long, but over days a streak also picks up everything else that drifts: your form, your rating, the pool you are being paired from. There is no way to tell those apart from the mood, so this study asks the narrower question the "stop after three losses" advice is about: what a streak does to your next game while you are still at the board. Keep only streaks that happened in one sitting.
  • Accounts. Players far from their usual level (second accounts, rating resets, three years of drift) are over-represented in long streaks. Drop each player's first 100 games in the record and any game played more than 150 points from their long-run rating.
  • The rematch. About one next game in ten is against someone you already played during the streak, usually the same opponent again and again. That is not a fresh draw from the pool: the matchup that produced the streak is still on the board, and the opponent is in a state of their own. Inside a rematch the streak effect is about twice as large. Keep only next games against a fresh opponent; the rematch gets its own treatment in section 6.

What survives all four: 48% after six losses, 53% after six wins. Even that is not all tilt. Within 100 points, the next game is still not quite an even pairing. Streaks tend to come from lopsided stretches, a run of games against people rated a little above you or a little below you, and the next game usually comes from the same stretch. So, relative to your own rating at that moment, the opponent after a losing streak is on average a few points above you and the one after a winning streak a few points below, and what your rating predicts for the next game is 50.3% and 51.0%, against 50.8% for an ordinary game in this sample (the dotted line is that prediction, and its zero point is the sample's own average, not 50%). The gap between the solid line and the dotted line is what is left: −2.0 points after losses, and after wins its mirror image, what sports science calls the hot hand: +1.7. Both are small. This story calls the gap after losses tilt for short, but it is a shortfall against a benchmark, not a reading of anyone's mood: fatigue, distraction, a bad connection and the fact that only players who kept playing are counted all feed into it.

2

What is left is small, and grows with the streak

#

Score minus expectation, one streak length at a time. A single loss costs 0.2 points, barely measurable. Two to four losses cost 0.5 to 0.8 points, five cost 1.6, six or more cost 2.0. The hot hand mirrors it: three straight wins add 1.0 points, six or more add 1.7. To put those numbers in perspective, after three straight losses you still win the next game 48.1% of the time and lose 47.9%, and even after six losses in a row the chance of losing the next one stays just under 50%.

The same picture holds when each player is compared only with themselves. A model that holds the player, the exact clock setting, the rating gap and the colour fixed puts the game after six or more losses 2.3 points below that player's game after a single loss, and the swing from six wins to six losses at 4.4 points (technical report, section 2d). Changing the definition of a sitting or the account filters moves the tails by a few tenths of a point.

3

Rapid players, and beginners, fall furthest

#

After three or more losses in a row, bullet players score 0.7 points below expectation, blitz players 0.3, rapid players 1.8. The hot hand is about the same in all three, +1.1 to +1.3. By rating, the shortfall is largest at the bottom: −1.9 at 800, then between 0 and −0.8 from 1200 all the way to 2400, with the hot hand at +0.7 to +1.5 everywhere. These are descriptions of groups, not of causes: rapid players and bullet players are different people playing at different clocks, and the intervals of neighbouring groups overlap.

4

Does a break help? The data cannot say.

#

The obvious remedy is to walk away, and the data on what happens next is awkward. Players who start their next game within a minute of a second straight loss score 0.1 points below expectation. Players who pause for 10–30 minutes score 2.4 below. That is not a causal claim: the player who steps away after two losses is often the one who is more upset, more tired or more distracted, and a ten-minute break is more likely to be a phone call than a meditation. The fairer comparison is the gap between players coming off wins and players coming off losses at the same break length: it is 1.5 to 2.5 points for any break under 30 minutes, and gone after 30 minutes to an hour. Even that gap compares different people, though (those who pause after a win and those who pause after a loss need not be alike), and at 30 to 60 minutes both groups sit below expectation, so it closes mostly because the winners come down, not because the losers recover. What the data cannot say is how long a streak's effect lasts for a given player, or what a break does to it; the half-hour cells are small enough that the point where the gap closes could sit anywhere between ten minutes and a couple of hours.

Two more things the data do say. Almost nobody stops: after one loss, 71% of players start another game within the hour, and after six or more losses 87%, mostly because long same-sitting streaks happen deep in long sessions. And bullet has a twist: playing straight on after two losses costs nothing (+0.1), a 3–10 minute pause is followed by −2.9, and the first game of any bullet session runs −2.1. A cold start costs a bullet player more than a losing streak ever does.

5

Which loss hurts the next game? Not the one you think.

#

Folklore says the blown win hurts most. Split losses by what they looked like and the data cannot find it. A loss in which you had entered the endgame two pawns up (a Stockfish evaluation of +2 or better, and still lost) is followed by a game at or above expectation (+0.4; +0.6 among long games). Two thirds of those are flags in a won endgame; the blown win by resignation, the kind that really stings, is rarer and its interval (−1.1 to +1.0) rules out anything large. A long fight of more than 30 moves: no effect at all (+0.1). What is followed by a worse game is the quick collapse. A loss in under eleven moves is followed by −2.1, a game that never reached an endgame by −1.2, and a loss by disconnection by −3.7. Resigning (−0.7) is worse than getting checkmated (−0.2) or flagged (−0.3).

A plausible reading is not that quick losses cause tilt but that they reveal something: a ten-move loss is often a distracted, pre-moving, phone-in-hand game, or a bad connection, and whatever it was is still there in the next one. One caveat: a game thrown away before the endgame, a hung queen at move 20, lands in the "never reached an endgame" cell with every other short loss, so this cut cannot tell that kind of blown win from a collapse. What it can say is that among players who kept playing, the game you fought hard and lost is not followed by a worse one.

6

What changes after a loss: faster, angrier, gone

#

If the score barely moves, where does the feeling go? Into behaviour. After a loss, players:

  • Speed up. Time per move in the opening and early middlegame (moves 3 to 20) falls 2 to 4% after a loss compared with after a win, comparing each player with themselves. It is not a few people driving the average: two thirds to three quarters of players are individually faster after a loss.
  • Blunder a little more, eventually, maybe. In rapid games with an engine evaluation (a Lichess analysis requested by either player, or Stockfish run by the study), 5.8 blunders per 100 moves after a loss and 5.7 after a win; after three or more losses, 6.3. The opponent's rate rises in those same games too, by about half as much, so part of this may be the kind of game rather than the player. On the clock the change shows after one loss; on the board, if at all, after three.
  • Quit, or rush. A bullet session ends after 22.3% of losses and 17.2% of wins; 55% of bullet sessions end on a loss, against a 48% loss rate. Rapid players who carry on do it faster after a loss: of those who play again within the hour, 51% start within a minute after a loss and 40% after a win (counting everyone, quitters included, 32% and 25%).
  • Take revenge. Sections 1 to 5 measured streaks against fresh opponents; here the rematch comes back. One next game in eleven is a rematch against the opponent who just won. Those games average 47.9% against an expectation of 49.7%: −1.8 points, against −0.5 for the next game against someone new, so the rematch itself costs about 1.3 points (1.5 for the first rematch of a pairing). How much of that is the matchup and how much is you, the data cannot split: the player who just beat you is, on the evidence of that game, a little better than their rating, and the mirror shows a matching bonus (the first rematch against someone you just beat runs +0.5 above a fresh opponent), but both players choose the rematch for reasons of their own. The penalty grows with the game length: −1.2 in bullet, −1.7 in blitz, −3.0 in rapid, −4.3 in classical.

What to take to your next session

#
  • Your stats overstate tilt. After three straight losses your next game runs 0.5 points below what your rating predicts, the equivalent of one win turned into a loss every 190 games; after six or more, one per 50. Your raw stats show a swing several times bigger, and that part is who you were playing and when, not you.
  • The stop-loss rule is untested, not disproven. Games played right after two losses in one sitting cost 0.07 points per 100 games in total, and whether stopping would recover any of that, no game history can show, because the games that stopped players would have played do not exist. What the data do show: in bullet, a cold start (2.1 points for the first game of a sitting) is bigger than any losing streak.
  • Think twice about the revenge rematch. The immediate rematch against the player who just beat you runs about 1.3 points below an ordinary post-loss game, more than four losses in a row; in rapid, about 2.3.
  • Do not fear the long loss. Losing a game you fought for, even from a won endgame, is not followed by a worse game on average. The losses that are: ten-move collapses and disconnects, which mostly tell you that you were not really playing, or not really connected, in the first place.
  • Watch the clock first. After a loss you play 2 to 4% faster and are more likely to quit or to rush into the next game; the blunder rate only moves, if at all, after three losses in a row. If you notice yourself pre-moving through the opening, that is what the data can see.

About this analysis

I analyzed 2,670,215 rated Lichess games played between March 2023 and May 2026 by the 4,487 players in the FlawChess benchmark database, roughly 200 players in each of twenty rating (800 to 2400) and time-control (bullet, blitz, rapid, classical) cells, about 300 games each. Each player's games were ordered in time within a time control; a break of an hour or more from the end of one game to the start of the next (game ends are reconstructed from the clock times in the game record; there is no end timestamp) starts a new session. Only games between opponents within 100 rating points are scored (78% of games; the sequence features, streaks, breaks and rematches, use the full history). Every streak number also requires a fresh opponent: the next game is against nobody from the streak, because a rematch series carries its own matchup and the streak effect inside one is about twice as large (rematches are analyzed on their own in section 6). "Expected" is the average score of players in the same time control, 400-point rating band, colour and 25-point rating-gap bin, measured on the same kind of games (settled accounts, not the first game of a sitting), because Lichess ratings are not quite Elo-calibrated: a 100 to 150-point favourite scores about 63%, not the 67% the Elo formula predicts. Every interval is a bootstrap over players, and for the main curve it also allows for the uncertainty in that expectation. A model that compares each player only with themselves, at the same clock setting and rating gap, reproduces the main curve. Every number describes players who kept playing within the hour; the players who stopped are, by construction, not observed, and nothing here measures mood directly. An earlier draft of this story was briefly online in September 2026 before a methods review; this version fixes a game-timing bug and withdraws the claims about stopping and about the share of the rematch penalty that is mood. Full methodology, all controls (colour, trailing form, the within-player model, alternative session and account rules), every table, and a comparison with the published literature are in the technical report.

Read the technical report · Browse the benchmark database

Data: Lichess players selected for having a long history in one time control, so this is a sample of regular players, not of everyone on Lichess. Everything here is observational: who continues, who takes a break and who accepts a rematch is self-selected, which is why the story leans on differences between groups facing the same selection (hot versus cold at the same break length) rather than on single cells. Two published studies that condition on ratings the way this one does (Gee et al. 2025 on Lichess, Rosenthal 2025 on chess.com) also find next-game effects of at most a few points; analyses reporting 10 to 20 point swings do not control for opponent rating or rating lag. Time of day is not analysed (the data has no player time zone). The post-loss dip differs between players, but weakly: estimates of a single player's own number from 300 games are about 90% noise (split-half reliability 0.11), so this story reports population averages rather than a personal tilt score. Unrated games and games against bots are excluded.
