You've lost three in a row.
Should you stop?

Every chess player knows the feeling: a couple of losses, and the next game already feels lost before it starts. Tilt has a name, a forum genre and a standard piece of advice attached to it, usually some version of "stop after two losses". So I took the FlawChess benchmark database, 2.67 million rated Lichess games from 4,487 players across bullet, blitz, rapid and classical, lined up each player's games in order, and looked at what actually happens in the game after a losing streak. The short version: tilt is real, it is far smaller than your own stats make it look, and the standard advice barely moves the needle.

1

Your stats say tilt is huge. They are wrong.

#

Take any player's game history and look at the game right after a streak. After six or more straight losses the next game scores 43%. After six or more straight wins, 65%. That is a 22-point swing, and it is the kind of number most articles about tilt report. Almost none of it is tilt. Three things inflate it:

  • Opponents. A long streak is partly a story about who you were paired with: the next opponent is on average 175 rating points weaker after six wins and 61 points stronger after six losses. Streaks happen against weak opposition, in rematch series against the same player, in arenas, and at the thin top of the pool. Keep only games against an opponent within 100 points and the swing shrinks to 48–54%.
  • The calendar. Seven in ten streaks of six or more span more than one sitting, and four in ten more than a day. That is a slump or a climb, not a state of mind. Keep only streaks that happened in one sitting.
  • Accounts. Players far from their usual level (second accounts, rating resets, three years of drift) are over-represented in long streaks. Drop each player's first 100 games in the record and any game played more than 150 points from their long-run rating.

What survives all three: 47% after six losses, 54% after six wins. Even that is not all tilt. The pairing system still leaves you slightly the weaker player after losses and slightly the stronger after wins, so what your rating predicts for the next game is 49.8% and 51.5%. The gap between the solid line and the dotted line is tilt, or its opposite: −2.6 and +2.6 points.

2

What is left is real, small, and grows with the streak

#

Score minus expectation, one streak length at a time. A single loss costs 0.3 points, barely measurable. Two to four losses cost 0.7 to 1.0 points, five cost 1.8, six or more cost 2.6. Wins mirror it: three straight wins add 1.2 points, six or more add 2.6. To put those numbers in perspective, after three straight losses you still win the next game 47.6% of the time and lose 48.4%. The odds of losing the next game only cross 50% at the sixth loss in a row.

3

Slow games tilt harder. Beginners tilt most.

#

After three or more losses in a row, bullet players score 0.9 points below expectation, blitz players 0.8, rapid players 1.9. The longer the game you just lost, the longer it stays with you. The hot hand does not care: +1.4 to +1.6 in all three. By rating, tilt is mostly a beginner's problem: −2.2 at 800, then flat at around −0.6 to −0.9 from 1200 all the way to 2400. The hot hand is the same size everywhere, +1.3 to +1.7.

4

Does a break help? Not a short one.

#

The obvious remedy is to walk away, and the data on what happens next is awkward. Players who start their next game within a minute of a second straight loss score 0.6 points below expectation. Players who pause for 10–30 minutes score 2.5 below. That is not a causal claim: the player who steps away after two losses is often the one who is more upset, more tired or more distracted, and a ten-minute break is more likely to be a phone call than a meditation. The honest comparison is the gap between players coming off wins and players coming off losses at the same break length: it is about two points for any break under 30 minutes, and gone after 30 minutes to an hour. The two sides do not fade the same way, though: the hot hand is gone after a break of a few minutes (+1.4 within a minute, +0.5 after one to three, nothing beyond ten), while the cold effect deepens for the first half hour. By the gap, whatever a streak does to you lasts about half an hour whether you pause or not; the half-hour cells are small enough that the true fade could sit anywhere between ten minutes and a couple of hours.

Bullet has a twist. Playing straight on after two losses costs 0.4 points, a 3–10 minute pause is followed by −2.9, and the first game of any bullet session runs −2.2. A cold start costs a bullet player more than tilt ever does.

5

Which loss tilts you? Not the one you think.

#

Folklore says the blown win hurts most. Split losses by what they looked like and the data cannot find it. A loss in which you had entered the endgame two pawns up (Stockfish +2 or better, and still lost) is followed by a game at expectation (+0.2; +0.5 among long games). Two thirds of those are flags in a won endgame; the blown win by resignation, the kind that really stings, is rarer and its interval (−1.3 to +0.5) rules out anything large. A long fight of more than 30 moves: no effect at all (−0.1). What hurts is the quick collapse. A loss in under eleven moves is followed by −2.1, a game that never reached an endgame by −1.3, and a loss by disconnection by −3.5. Resigning (−0.9) is worse than getting checkmated (−0.3) or flagged (−0.4).

A plausible reading is not that quick losses cause tilt but that they reveal it: a ten-move loss is often a distracted, pre-moving, phone-in-hand game, and that state carries into the next one. One caveat: a game thrown away before the endgame, a hung queen at move 20, lands in the "never reached an endgame" cell with every other short loss, so this cut cannot tell that kind of blown win from a collapse. What it can say is that the game you fought hard and lost is not the one to worry about.

6

What tilt actually looks like: faster, angrier, gone

#

If the score barely moves, where does the feeling go? Into behaviour. After a loss, players:

  • Speed up. Time per move in the opening and early middlegame (moves 3 to 20) falls 2 to 4% after a loss compared with after a win, for two thirds to three quarters of all players.
  • Blunder a little more, eventually. In rapid games with full engine analysis, 6.4 blunders per 100 moves after a loss and 6.4 after a win; after three or more losses, 7.0. The opponent's rate rises in those same games, so part of it is the sharper, faster game a tilted player gets into. Tilt shows up on the clock after one loss, and on the board after three.
  • Quit, or rush. A bullet session ends after 21.7% of losses and 16.2% of wins; 55% of bullet sessions end on a loss, against a 47% loss rate. Rapid players do the opposite: 53% start the next game within a minute after a loss, 43% after a win.
  • Take revenge. One next game in eleven is a rematch against the opponent who just won. Those games score 47.9% against an expectation of 49.7%: −1.7 points, against −0.5 for the next game against someone new, so the rematch itself costs about 1.2 points. Some of that is the matchup: the player who just beat you is, on the evidence of that game, a little better than their rating, and the mirror shows it (the first rematch against someone you just beat runs +0.5 above a fresh opponent). The rest, roughly a point, is the mood. The revenge penalty grows with the game length: −1.1 in bullet, −1.7 in blitz, −2.9 in rapid, −4.0 in classical.

What to take to your next session

#
  • Tilt is real, and small. After three straight losses your next game runs 0.8 points below what your rating predicts, about one extra loss per 120 games; after six or more, one per 40. Your raw stats show a swing four times bigger, and that part is who you were paired with and when, not you.
  • Forget the stop-loss rule. Stopping after every second loss saves about one point per thousand games. Whatever a streak does to you fades in about half an hour whether you pause or not, and in bullet a cold start costs more than tilt does: if anything, keep playing.
  • Decline the revenge rematch. The immediate rematch against the player who just beat you is the one tilt decision with a visible price: about a point beyond an ordinary post-loss game, as much as three losses in a row. In rapid it is two points.
  • Do not fear the long loss. Losing a game you fought for, even from a won endgame, leaves no mark on the next one. The losses that do are the ten-move collapses and the disconnects, which mostly tell you that you were not really playing in the first place.
  • Watch the clock first. After a loss you play 2 to 4% faster and are more likely to quit or to rush into the next game; the blunder rate only moves after three losses in a row. If you notice yourself pre-moving through the opening, that is what tilt looks like in the data.

About this analysis

I analyzed 2,670,215 rated Lichess games played between March 2023 and May 2026 by the 4,487 players in the FlawChess benchmark database, roughly 200 players in each of twenty rating (800 to 2400) and time-control (bullet, blitz, rapid, classical) cells, about 300 games each. Each player's games were ordered in time within a time control; a break of an hour or more from the end of one game to the start of the next (game ends come from the clock times in the game record) starts a new session. Only games between opponents within 100 rating points are scored (78% of games; the sequence features, streaks, breaks and rematches, use the full history). "Expected" is the average score of players in the same time control, 400-point rating band, colour and 25-point rating-gap bin, measured on the same kind of games (settled accounts, not the first game of a sitting), because Lichess ratings are not quite Elo-calibrated: a 100 to 150-point favourite scores about 63%, not the 67% the Elo formula predicts. Every interval is a bootstrap over players. Every tilt number describes players who kept playing within the hour; the players who stopped are, by construction, not observed. Full methodology, all controls (colour, trailing form, player fixed effects), every table, and a comparison with the published literature are in the technical report.

Read the technical report · Browse the benchmark database

Data: Lichess players selected for having a long history in one time control, so this is a sample of regular players, not of everyone on Lichess. Everything here is observational: who continues, who takes a break and who accepts a rematch is self-selected, which is why the story leans on differences between groups facing the same selection (hot versus cold at the same break length) rather than on single cells. Two published studies that condition on ratings the way this one does (Gee et al. 2025 on Lichess, Rosenthal 2025 on chess.com) also find next-game effects of at most a few points; analyses reporting 10 to 20 point swings do not control for opponent rating or rating lag. Time of day is not analysed (the data has no player time zone). Tilt-proneness differs between players, but weakly: a player's own tilt number from 300 games is about 80% noise (split-half reliability 0.19), so this story reports population averages rather than a personal tilt score. Unrated games and games against bots are excluded.
